Chapter notes

1. Status, prospects and constraints of aquaculture

Definition. Aquaculture is the farming of aquatic organisms involving intervention in the rearing process (such as stocking, feeding, protection or husbandry) and individual or corporate ownership of the stock being cultivated.

Scope and contribution

  • Produces fish, crustaceans, molluscs, aquatic plants, seed and other aquatic products.
  • Supports food and nutrition security, employment, income, export earnings and livelihoods.
  • Can complement capture fisheries; it does not automatically replace or eliminate pressure on wild stocks.

Major constraints

  • Biological: disease, poor seed quality, genetic deterioration, slow growth and limited domestication.
  • Environmental: water scarcity, pollution, salinity shifts, extreme weather, habitat loss and carrying-capacity limits.
  • Economic: feed and energy costs, credit, price volatility, market access and infrastructure.
  • Governance: access rights, biosecurity, licensing, traceability and compliance with discharge/environmental standards.

Exam distinction: production volume, production value, productivity (e.g. kg ha⁻¹ crop⁻¹) and profitability are different measures. Always attach a year, geography, species group and source to statistics.

2. Culture systems and production intensity

Culture systems are classified by water source/exchange, enclosure, stocking and management intensity, species combination and production environment. These classifications overlap; for example, a cage is an enclosure type, while intensive is a management-intensity class.

SystemCore featureMain consideration
ExtensiveLow external input; relies substantially on natural productivityLower control and generally lower yield per unit area
Semi-intensiveNatural food supplemented by inputs such as fertiliser and feedBalance nutrient input, oxygen and pond productivity
IntensiveHigh stocking and formulated-feed dependence; close controlHigh aeration, monitoring, biosecurity and waste-management demand

Enclosures: ponds are earthen or lined impoundments; tanks are bounded vessels; raceways use controlled directional flow; cages hold stock in open water; pens enclose a portion of a natural water body while retaining the bottom; rafts and ropes support suspended culture.

Species specificity: temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, stocking density and feeding rate must be selected for the species, life stage, system and local conditions. There is no universal stocking density.

3. Integrated farming, IMTA, aquaponics and biofloc

Integrated farming links aquaculture with crops or livestock so that water, nutrients, by-products or labour are used across components. Integration requires nutrient budgeting and safeguards against pathogen transfer, pesticide residues and oxygen depletion.

Polyculture rears two or more compatible species in the same unit, often to use different food niches. Mixed culture is a broad term for more than one species; it does not necessarily imply deliberate trophic complementarity.

IMTA combines fed species with extractive species. Inorganic extractive organisms (e.g. seaweeds) assimilate dissolved nutrients; organic extractive organisms (e.g. filter-feeding bivalves or deposit feeders) use particulate organic matter. It requires suitable hydrodynamics, species compatibility and monitoring.

Aquaponics couples aquatic animal production with hydroponic plant production. Microbial nitrification converts toxic ammonia to nitrite and then nitrate; plant uptake and solids removal support water quality. The plant, fish and nitrifying microbes have partly different optimum conditions.

Biofloc technology promotes microbial aggregates that assimilate/recycle nitrogenous wastes. Carbon addition may adjust the carbon:nitrogen balance, but the correct dose depends on feed nitrogen, carbon source and system conditions. Strong aeration, mixing, alkalinity and solids management are essential. Biofloc is not a substitute for monitoring.

4. Seaweed, molluscan and pearl culture

Seaweed farming cultivates marine macroalgae for food, hydrocolloids, feed, fertiliser and other products. Common methods include off-bottom monoline, raft and long-line systems. Site choice considers light, depth, water movement, nutrients, salinity, grazing, epiphytes, storms and access.

Commercial examples include Kappaphycus alvarezii (carrageenan), Gracilaria spp. (agar) and Gelidiella spp. (agar). Product yield and quality depend on species/strain, season, site and post-harvest handling. Do not treat all seaweeds as interchangeable.

Molluscan farming includes oysters, mussels, clams and scallops. Methods include bottom culture, rack-and-raft, stake, rope/long-line and suspended lantern nets. Many bivalves are filter feeders; carrying capacity, harmful algal blooms, microbial contamination, depuration and food-safety controls are important.

Pearl culture in pearl oysters involves implantation of a nucleus and/or mantle graft by trained technicians, followed by nacre deposition. Pearl quality is affected by host species, graft/nucleus quality, operation, husbandry and grow-out conditions. Natural pearls and cultured pearls are distinct; cultured pearls are not simply imitation products.

5. Ornamental fish culture

Ornamental aquaculture includes broodstock maintenance, breeding, larval/juvenile rearing, grow-out, grading, conditioning, quarantine, packaging and transport. Freshwater and marine ornamental species differ substantially in reproduction, salinity, live-feed needs and disease risks.

Examples of freshwater ornamentals include guppy (Poecilia reticulata), molly (Poecilia spp.), goldfish (Carassius auratus), koi carp (Cyprinus carpio) and selected cichlids. Marine ornamentals include clownfishes (e.g. Amphiprion spp.) and selected reef-associated species.

Important practices: maintain stable species-appropriate water quality; quarantine new stock; avoid mixing incompatible species; use suitable live feeds for larvae where required; grade to reduce size inequality/cannibalism; maintain records and biosecurity. Colour enhancement must not compromise welfare or involve prohibited substances.

Transport risk is influenced by duration, temperature, oxygen, biomass, water quality and handling. Fasting, packing density and oxygen management must be species- and journey-specific.

6. Inland saline and underground saline-water aquaculture

Inland saline aquaculture uses saline or brackish groundwater away from the coast, often where conventional freshwater farming is constrained. It can create productive use of otherwise underutilised land and water, but suitability is site-specific.

Characterise salinity (g L⁻¹ or ppt), ionic composition, alkalinity, hardness, pH, dissolved oxygen, major ions and potentially harmful constituents. Two waters with the same total salinity may differ in ionic balance and biological suitability.

Management options include species selection, acclimation, blending where lawful and sustainable, soil assessment, drainage and salt management. Evaluate groundwater abstraction, disposal of saline effluent, soil salinisation, energy cost and impacts on neighbouring users before development.

Species candidates must be chosen from validated local evidence; do not assume that every euryhaline fish or shrimp performs equally in every inland saline water.

Unit 1 revision sheet

  • Extensive: low external inputs; natural productivity is important.
  • Semi-intensive: natural productivity plus supplementary inputs.
  • Intensive: high input/stocking; high monitoring and life-support requirements.
  • Flow-through: water passes through the unit; effluent management matters.
  • RAS: treatment and reuse; mechanical filtration, biofiltration and oxygen management are central.
  • IMTA: fed species + inorganic and/or organic extractive species.
  • Aquaponics: aquatic animal culture + hydroponic plants + microbial nutrient conversion.
  • Biofloc: managed microbial aggregates; aeration, mixing, alkalinity and solids control are essential.
  • Bivalves: filter feeding creates production opportunities and food-safety responsibilities.
  • Inland saline: assess ionic composition, soil, groundwater sustainability and effluent disposal—not salinity alone.

Formula and unit reminders

  • Yield per crop = harvested biomass (kg) ÷ culture area (ha) = kg ha⁻¹ crop⁻¹.
  • Survival (%) = harvested number ÷ number stocked × 100.
  • SGR (% day⁻¹) = [ln(final weight) − ln(initial weight)] ÷ culture days × 100.
  • Always state the basis and units; use species- and system-specific target ranges from validated references.
